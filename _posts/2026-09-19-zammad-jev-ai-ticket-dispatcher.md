---
title: "Teaching Zammad to dispatch its own tickets with Jev"
---

I spend a fair amount of time around Zammad, the open source helpdesk, mostly on the infrastructure side: I help maintain the official `zammad-docker-compose` project, so I end up thinking about how people actually run this thing in production more than how they use it day to day. A few weeks ago I set up a fresh instance to model something specific: a small MSP, one team, several client organizations, all funneling into the same queue. And the first thing that stood out, the way it always does once you look at a real support queue instead of a demo one, was triage.

Every ticket that lands in that inbox needs a human to read it and decide which team it belongs to. VPN issue, that's network. Invoice question, that's billing. Printer jam, that's a desk visit. It's not hard work, but it's constant, it's the first thing that happens to every single request, and it's exactly the kind of decision that doesn't need a person making it forty times a day. So I built a dispatcher that makes it for them.

## What actually decides, and what doesn't

The model doing the classifying is Jev, from TypeSafe AI, and I think it's worth being precise about what that is, because the name alone doesn't tell you much and most people's first assumption, mine included at first, is "a faster chatbot." It isn't built for conversation at all. Jev answers one structured question at a time, a choice between fixed options, a score against described levels, or a calibrated yes/no, and it returns that answer with a confidence value attached, a real probability distribution, not a hedge. You're not parsing a sentence out of a reply and hoping it matches one of your categories. You get `{"choice": "Network & Security", "confidence": 0.94}` back, a value your code can act on directly. That distinction is the entire reason this project was practical to build in an afternoon instead of a week of prompt tuning: there was no prompt to tune. I wrote one instruction and handed it a list of groups with descriptions, and the criteria list is the only thing that has ever needed adjusting since.

## The actual wiring

The flow is small on purpose. A Trigger inside Zammad fires on every ticket that lands in the default inbox group and calls a Webhook, signed with HMAC so nothing else can hit the endpoint and start moving tickets around. That webhook hits a small service I wrote, which reads the ticket, asks Jev which of the configured service groups it belongs to, and if the confidence clears a threshold, moves the ticket there through Zammad's own API and leaves an internal note explaining the decision and the full probability breakdown. If the confidence doesn't clear the threshold, the ticket just stays put for a person to look at. That fallback mattered more to me than the happy path, honestly: a dispatcher that's occasionally wrong with total confidence is worse than the manual process it replaces, and the whole point of asking for a calibrated score instead of a label is that it tells you when to get out of the way.

The bridge between the two systems is a Sinatra app. I've written a fair amount of Ruby on Rails over the years, and there's a specific pleasure in reaching for Sinatra instead when the job is genuinely small: one route that verifies a signature, fetches a group list, makes one HTTP call, and writes a decision back. No framework ceremony, no models, just the shape of the problem in about a hundred and fifty lines. It's the kind of task Ruby has always been quietly good at, gluing two APIs together without either of them noticing there's a translator in the middle.

## Where it landed

I open sourced the result, [zammad-jev-dispatcher](https://github.com/enderkus/zammad-jev-dispatcher), MIT licensed, with a setup script that provisions the Zammad side (the groups, the API token, the webhook, the trigger) from a single config file, because the whole thing is useless to anyone else if setting it up means reading my shell history. The service groups and their descriptions live in one YAML file, and that file is, in a very literal sense, the entire configuration surface for how Jev routes your tickets. Rewrite a description, change what gets routed where. No redeploy, no retraining.

I'll be honest about the limits here too. This is a dispatcher, not a resolver, it decides who should look at a ticket, it doesn't answer the ticket. And it's only as good as the group descriptions you give it: vague categories get vague routing, however good the underlying model is. But for the specific, narrow, extremely common problem of "who should even see this first," it removed a step that never needed a person in the first place, and that's a satisfying thing to delete from a workflow.
